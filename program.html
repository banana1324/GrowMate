Our Program

GrowMate™ HEART PBL Program

Real projects become the classroom. Children build ability by doing the work, working with people, and reflecting on what happened.

What It Is

One small group. One real project each month.

Children are matched with three same-age peers. Each month, the group works through one PBL project built around a real driving question.

A role model mentor guides the group through the GrowLoop™ cycle: inquire, design, create, revise, present, and reflect.

What Children Practice

The GrowLoop™ cycle

The same six steps repeat across different projects. That repetition is intentional.

1

Look at the world with questions

Inquire

Children research, observe, interview, compare sources, and decide what evidence they trust. The goal is to get beyond the first easy answer.

HEART™: Thinking & Creativity · Action & Problem Solving
2

Make a choice and plan around it

Design

Children decide how they want to approach the work, choose a format, and make a plan. They have to stand behind the choice they make.

HEART™: Action & Problem Solving · Thinking & Creativity
3

Build something real

Create

Plans meet reality. Children make, test, adjust, and keep going when the first version does not work the way they expected.

HEART™: Action & Problem Solving · Resilience & Self-management
4

Use feedback without losing ownership

Revise

Peers ask questions and give feedback. Children decide what is useful, what is not, and how to improve the work.

HEART™: Human Collaboration · Thinking & Creativity · Resilience & Self-management
5

Explain the work to a real audience

Present

Children present what they made, explain their choices, and answer questions from peers and family.

HEART™: Expression & Communication · Human Collaboration
6

Look back and name what changed

Reflect

Children identify what they are proud of, what was difficult, how they responded, and what they learned from other people.

HEART™: Resilience & Self-management · Thinking & Creativity
At the end of the month, the main result is not a certificate. It is a completed piece of work, a real experience, and a clearer picture of how the child handled it.

Three Pillars

What supports the monthly cycle

🔨

Real Projects

Projects span science, technology, data, society, history, arts, entrepreneurship, and critical thinking. The questions are designed to cross subject boundaries.

Children have to decide what information matters and how to turn it into something they can explain.

👥

Peer Groups

Four same-age children share progress, respond to one another, and meet at the monthly showcase.

Peers give the work a social context. Children practice listening, feedback, responsibility, and collaboration.

🧭

Role Model Mentors

Mentors guide without taking over. They ask questions, give enough support to keep the work moving, and observe how the child approaches the project.

As children grow, mentors step back and give them more ownership.

Who It's For

Three learner levels

The structure stays familiar while the level of independence, scale, and responsibility grows with age.

🔭 Explorers

Ages 6-9

Concrete, hands-on, and close to everyday life.

Projects have clear steps and enough structure to keep children moving, while leaving room for their own ideas.

Mentors provide more support at this level and help children build the habits needed to work in a small group.

Parent role: companion and observer. Help with logistics, notice effort, and let the child own the work.

🏗 Builders

Ages 10-13

More ownership, stronger peer influence, wider questions.

Projects begin to move beyond the home and into the community. Children make more of the decisions and deal with more open-ended work.

Mentors act more like coaches. They push the thinking without solving the problem for the child.

Parent role: supporter. Provide resources when needed, then step back.

🚀 Innovators

Ages 14-17

Independent work on larger questions and real audiences.

Projects can address city-scale or global questions. Students take more responsibility for direction, research, production, and presentation.

Mentors move toward an advisory role and focus on questions, feedback, and reflection.

Parent role: witness. Offer support without managing the project.
